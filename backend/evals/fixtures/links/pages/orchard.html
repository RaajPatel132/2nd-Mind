A year in the old orchard

The orchard at the end of Hollow Lane was planted more than a century ago, and most of its trees have outlived the families who tended them. In January the branches are bare and the work is pruning: opening up the crowns so that light and air can reach the centre, and cutting out the crossed and rubbing limbs that would otherwise wound each other in the wind.

February brings the grafting. Scions cut from the healthiest old trees are bound onto young rootstocks with a strip of waxed cloth, and each one is labelled in pencil with the variety. Some of the names are older than anyone can explain, such as Winter Banana, Sheep's Nose and the small russet that everyone simply calls the Hollow apple.

By April the blossom arrives, first on the plums and then, a fortnight later, on the apples. The beekeeper down the lane brings three hives and sets them at the field's edge, because a cold spring with few bees can cost half the crop. Frost is the great fear, and on clear nights the volunteers light smoky fires of damp straw between the rows.

Early summer is the quiet season. The fruit is small and green and the grass between the trees is scythed twice, leaving the clippings under the branches as mulch. Sheep are let in for a few weeks to graze the rest, and they are taken out again well before the fruit begins to ripen so that nothing is nibbled or fouled.

In July the trees shed some of their young fruit by themselves, a natural thinning that the old hands call the June drop. Whatever the trees do not drop is thinned by hand so that the remaining apples grow larger and do not weigh down the branches. It is slow work, done from a ladder with a pair of small scissors.

August is for repairs. The wooden crates are scrubbed, the ladders are checked for loose rungs, and the picking bags are mended. The old barn, which holds the press and the storage racks, gets a new coat of limewash so that it stays cool and clean through the autumn rush.

September begins the harvest with the earliest varieties, which do not keep and are eaten or pressed within days. The pickers work in pairs, one on the ladder and one holding the bag, and every apple is lifted rather than pulled so that the stalk stays on. Bruised fruit goes into a separate crate for juice.

October is the busiest month. The late keepers are picked on the last dry days and laid out in single layers in the apple store, where they will slowly sweeten until Christmas. Each rack is checked weekly, and any fruit that has started to rot is removed at once so that the others are not spoiled.

Pressing day is a village event. Crates of mixed fruit are tipped into the mill, ground to a pulp, and built into layers of cloth and pomace called a cheese, which is then squeezed under the press until the juice runs gold and cloudy into the tub below. Children are allowed to taste the first cup, and the rest is carried home in borrowed bottles.

The press itself has its own story. The cider press in the barn was restored in 1978 by a retired carpenter who cut a new oak screw for it entirely by hand, after the original cracked in a hard frost. The job took him the whole winter, and the screw is still turning today, with his initials cut into the top of the wooden beam.

In November the last of the juice is left to ferment slowly in barrels in the cold end of the barn, and the trees drop their leaves. The volunteers gather for a supper in the barn to share the first bottles of the previous year's cider and to plan the next round of grafting, and the cycle begins again.
